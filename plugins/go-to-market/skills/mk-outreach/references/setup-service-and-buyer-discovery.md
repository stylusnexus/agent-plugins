# Offering a paid setup service to learn what buyers actually need

For an early product, doing one customer's setup by hand — before there's a generalized, self-serve version of anything — is not a lesser version of the business. It's how the first real evidence about what buyers need gets collected.

## Why manual, one-at-a-time work comes first

Y Combinator's Paul Graham argues that early growth almost never arrives on its own: "the most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually." His own example is Airbnb, going door to door in New York "recruiting new users and helping existing ones improve their listings" — he calls marketplaces so hard to get rolling that founders "should expect to take heroic measures at first." He also argues that how a startup is doing months later "will depend more on how happy you made those users than how many there were of them," which is the case for doing a small number of setups well over chasing volume early. Source: Paul Graham, "Do Things that Don't Scale" — https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

## The same idea has a name in product management: concierge MVP

A concierge MVP delivers the outcome a customer wants through "manual, high-touch interactions to simulate the envisioned user experience," with a person standing in for the automated features that don't exist yet. It's distinct from a "Wizard of Oz" MVP specifically on disclosure: "the concierge MVP assumes you're open about this being a manual service," where a Wizard of Oz MVP "feels automated but is secretly human-powered." The point isn't to look automated; it's to learn cheaply and quickly whether the problem is real, whether someone will pay to have it solved, and what the actual workflow looks like before committing engineering time to automating the wrong thing. Source: Upsilon, "What Is a Concierge MVP and When to Use It" — https://www.upsilonit.com/blog/what-is-a-concierge-mvp-and-when-to-use-it

## Applying this to a design-partner or early-customer offer

Offer to install, configure, or run the thing by hand for one real partner, rather than pitching a packaged, self-serve product that doesn't exist yet. Concretely:

- Frame the ask as hands-on help with their specific problem, not a product demo — the software (if any exists yet) is incidental to solving their actual pain this week.
- Do the setup work yourself the first few times, even where it clearly won't scale, because that's where the real requirements show up — the feature nobody asked for in a survey but that broke the first real attempt.
- Only generalize a piece of the manual work into a repeatable, packaged offer once the same request has come up with more than one partner — treat repetition, not a single strong request, as the signal that it's worth building.
- Keep asking what's actually blocking the partner day to day; a paid setup relationship gives an honest reason to keep asking, because the partner has skin in the outcome too.
