# Asking someone you know for an introduction

A warm introduction beats cold outreach because someone the target already trusts is vouching, not because it's a cleverer message. Two practices from startup-network writing cover most of what makes an ask land well or badly.

## Ask permission on both sides before connecting anyone

Investor Fred Wilson named this the "double opt-in introduction": before connecting two people who don't know each other, ask each one separately whether they're willing to be introduced, and only send the actual introduction once both have said yes. He proposed it because an un-opted-in intro puts the recipient in an awkward spot — "all of us get email intros that we don't want to follow up on," and either they take a meeting they didn't want or they ignore or stall someone they had no way to say no to first. The same logic applies in reverse: when asking a connector to introduce *you* to someone, ask them to check with the other person first rather than assuming a forward is welcome. Source: Fred Wilson, "The Double Opt-In Introduction," AVC — https://avc.com/2009/11/the-double-optin-introduction/

## Write the introduction yourself, so the connector only has to hit forward

Writing a full introduction "properly" costs the connector real time — investor Alex Iskold, writing about this practice from his own experience making introductions, put it at roughly ten minutes done well. A forwardable email removes that cost: write the note addressed directly to the person you want to meet, explaining who you are, what the company does, and the concrete reason you want to connect (traction, a specific shared context, a specific ask) — then send it to the connector and ask them only to forward it, adding a sentence or two in their own words about you. The final recipient then sees the connector's short vouching note on top, with your own words underneath, and can reply straight to you. Source: Alex Iskold, "How to write a forwardable introduction email" — https://www.alexiskold.net/2015/06/24/how-to-write-a-forwardable-introduction-email/

## Applying both together

1. Identify a person the target already trusts — someone who has worked with them, serves them in a professional capacity, or is active in the same community — rather than the most senior name available.
2. Ask that connector, separately, whether they'd be willing to make the introduction at all, before drafting anything (double opt-in, applied to the connector's own consent).
3. Draft the forwardable email yourself: addressed to the target, in your own voice, with the specific reason this introduction makes sense for them right now.
4. Send it to the connector with an easy out — "no worries at all if this isn't a fit for you to send" — and let them decide whether and how to forward it.
5. If the connector agrees to check with the target first (the double opt-in step), wait for that confirmation before anything goes to the target directly.

## Where this fits with cold outreach

Not every target has a real warm path. When none exists, use [outreach-method.md](outreach-method.md) for a direct cold message instead of stretching a thin connection to manufacture one — a forced or unwilling introduction damages the connector's credibility with the target more than a well-written cold message would.
