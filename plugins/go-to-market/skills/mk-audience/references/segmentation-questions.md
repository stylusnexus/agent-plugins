# Choosing how to segment an audience

Segmenting means picking which factor (or factors) to group customers by, out of several possible bases — and deciding how many groups are worth treating differently. Both choices should be answerable from evidence, not defaulted to "everyone."

## The bases available

Classic marketing segmentation works from a small set of bases: geographic (where they are), demographic (who they are, by measurable trait), psychographic (attitudes, values, lifestyle), and behavioral (how they actually act toward the product — usage, occasion, loyalty). Source: OpenStax, *Principles of Marketing*, "5.1 Market Segmentation and Consumer Markets" — https://openstax.org/books/principles-marketing/pages/5-1-market-segmentation-and-consumer-markets

B2B audiences add company-level bases on top of these: firmographic (industry, size, revenue, geography), technographic (what tools or stack they already run), and behavioral/needs-based (what they've tried, what job they're hiring the product for — see [icp.md](icp.md)). A useful segmentation usually combines more than one base rather than relying on firmographics alone, since company size or industry rarely explains the sale by itself. Source: aggregated B2B segmentation guides — https://salesintel.io/blog/firmographic-segmentation/

## How many segments, and how big

There's no fixed right number. Decision Analyst, a market-research firm that publishes on segmentation methodology, sets a size floor rather than a target count: "we recommend having at least 100 consumers/decision-makers in each segment," specifically to prevent "key business decisions being made based on segments that are too small." Beyond size, its own bar for a good segmentation solution is that it has "face validity," is "easy to explain," and "contain[s] segments that are reachable" with "clearly defined targets" — the final number of segments should be an outcome of finding a well-differentiated split, not a count decided in advance. Source: Decision Analyst, "How Many Segments Are Optimal?" — https://www.decisionanalyst.com/blog/how-many-segments-are-optimal/

## A real example: segmenting by role instead of company

GitLab publishes its own marketing personas openly in its public handbook, filed under "User Personas" and separately under "Buyer Personas." Two of its user personas alone show role-based segmentation inside the same company: "Sasha," a Software Developer, and "Delaney," a Development Team Lead — both classified by GitLab as user personas, each attached to a different day-to-day job and a different reason to care about the product. This is a real, public instance of segmenting primarily by role and behavior rather than by firmographics alone — the same company can be the right account while still needing two different messages for two different people inside it. Source: GitLab Handbook, "Delaney Persona snippets" (listed under User Personas) — https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/marketing/brand-and-product-marketing/product-and-solution-marketing/persona-snippets/user-personas/delaney/ ; GitLab Handbook, User Personas index — https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/marketing/brand-and-product-marketing/product-and-solution-marketing/persona-snippets/user-personas/ (© GitLab; the handbook page itself is the source of record, not a reproduction here)

## Applying it

For the audience in front of you: name which base or combination of bases the evidence actually supports (don't assume psychographic or behavioral data exists if nobody's talked to a real customer yet), check each candidate segment against the reachability/distinctness/size bar above, and stop adding segments once a new one wouldn't change the message or the channel. Revisit the split when a new segment shows up unprompted in real signups, or when one segment's behavior stops looking like the rest.
