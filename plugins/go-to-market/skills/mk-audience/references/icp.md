# Defining an ideal customer profile

An ideal customer profile (ICP) is a description of the account or person most likely to buy, get value from, and stay with the product — built from evidence about real customers, not a guessed persona. Two public bodies of work cover the pieces worth combining: standard B2B segmentation categories (firmographic, technographic, and behavioral criteria) and Jobs to Be Done (why they'd actually buy).

## What to describe, and with what evidence

**Firmographics** — the company-level facts: industry, size (employee count or revenue), geography, business model. These are the easiest facts to verify and the weakest predictor on their own; they narrow the list, they don't explain the sale. Source: aggregated B2B segmentation guides — https://salesintel.io/blog/firmographic-segmentation/

**Technographics** — what the account already uses: their existing tools, stack, or workflow. This matters because it points at integration opportunities, a specific tool being displaced, or a maturity level the product assumes. Source: same aggregated guides — https://ai-ark.com/blog/firmographic-data

**Behavioral evidence** — what the account or person has actually done: how they currently solve this problem, what they've tried and abandoned, what they engage with. This is the strongest evidence and the hardest to get without real conversations or a close read of where they already complain about the problem.

**The job, not the demographic** — Jobs to Be Done treats the customer as someone trying to make progress on something, and separates the job into functional, social, and emotional dimensions (the practical task; how the choice looks to others involved; what they're personally trying to feel or avoid feeling). Two people who look identical firmographically can be hiring the product for different jobs; two people in different industries can be hiring it for the same one. Source: Christensen Institute, "Jobs to Be Done Theory" — https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/

## Buying triggers and disqualifiers (INFERENCE)

This suite's own addition, not drawn from either sourced body of work above: alongside who they are and what job they're hiring the product for, it's useful to also name what specific event or change makes them ready to act now (a trigger), and what rules someone out even if the rest fits (a disqualifier — too small to need it, already locked into a multi-year contract, a compliance requirement the product can't meet). Both come from the same evidence sources as the behavioral row, not from guessing.

## Where the evidence comes from

Every field above needs a real source: a direct conversation, a support thread, a sales call note, a forum or community post in the customer's own words. A field with no source is a hypothesis, not a finding, and should be marked as one rather than written as fact — this suite's own convention (`MISSING INFORMATION` / `ASSUMPTION`) applies here the same as everywhere else in the workflow. Fill in what's known; name what isn't.

## Getting the evidence first

When there's too little evidence to fill the categories above with real sources, get it before drafting anything: talk directly to a handful of people who plausibly fit the rough target (five is a workable, achievable number for a small team, not a hard minimum), or do a close read of the threads, reviews, or support tickets where people already describe this problem in their own words. Either source produces the same thing — direct quotes and specific situations to fill the behavioral and job-based fields above, rather than a guess at what they probably think.

## Revisiting

An ICP drifts as real customers accumulate — the profile of who actually buys and sticks is often narrower, or different, from the first guess. Revisit it whenever a new signup or customer doesn't match the current profile, whenever a segment churns disproportionately, or before a positioning or segmentation decision that depends on it.
