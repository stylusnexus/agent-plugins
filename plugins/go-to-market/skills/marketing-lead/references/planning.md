# Planning marketing for a small team

The goal → audience → message → channel sequence below runs in the same order as the UK Government Communication Service's OASIS framework — "a five-step framework all government communicators should use to plan, deliver and evaluate their communications": Objectives, then Audience and Insights, then Strategy and Ideas, then Implementation, then Scoring and Evaluation. Source: Government Communications, "OASIS: planning government communications" — https://www.communications.gov.uk/publication/oasis-planning-government-communications/

## 1. Anchor the plan to one number
Start from a single outcome, not a wish list. Pick one measurable goal for the next 4–8 weeks, tied to revenue or a validation milestone — "60 qualified signups who've completed onboarding," not "more awareness." Once that number is fixed, test every later decision against it: does this activity move that specific number, or does it just look like marketing activity?

Filling in the plan means working out what the goal actually depends on:
- **Who has to take the action that produces the number** — find and describe them (`mk-audience`).
- **What would make them take it, in language they'd actually use** — the underlying claim (`mk-positioning`) and the words that carry it (`mk-copy`).
- **Where they'll actually run into that message** — `mk-search` for owned/found channels, `mk-community` and `mk-launch` for running it.

Every activity in the plan has to trace back to the one goal by name. An activity that can't answer "which part of the goal does this move" gets cut, however good it looks on its own.

## 2. Lead with owned and earned channels; keep outreach secondary
For a small team, the highest-leverage channel is usually the one that costs time, not money: posting where the audience already reads, replying to every comment, and joining conversations where someone is already asking for exactly this (`mk-founder-content`, `mk-community`). The replies matter more than the posts — "post and wait" is the common mistake; a post with no follow-up teaches you nothing about whether it worked.
- **Primary — conversational, owned content:** founder or team posts, comment replies, and participation in threads where the problem is already being discussed.
- **Compounding:** anything that keeps working after it's published — pages that get found and cited, a README people share, an email list (`mk-search`, `mk-lifecycle`).
- **Secondary — direct outreach:** cold or warm outreach, design-partner invitations (`mk-outreach`), and any paid or printed materials. Plan these for a specific person, event, or partner rather than as a standing weekly activity; treat print materials (business cards, event handouts) as a one-off purchase that needs a decision-maker's approval, not a default line item.

## 3. Narrow to what the team can actually finish
Resist running every tactic at once. Once the goal, audience, and channels from steps 1–2 are set, cut the list down further, on purpose:
- **One goal.** Restate it from step 1 — if the plan is quietly serving two goals at once, pick the one that matters more right now and park the other.
- **One audience.** The specific group from step 1's first question, not "everyone who might be interested."
- **The fewest channels that reach that audience,** not the most channels available — every channel added is hours taken from the ones already chosen.
- **A weekly check.** A fixed short slot (see §4) to look at what shipped against the goal's metric and decide, honestly, whether to keep going, adjust, or stop — not a retrospective on effort spent.

The output of this step is the same plan from step 1, cut down to what fits in the hours from §4, not a new list of tactics layered on top of it.

## 4. Fit it into real hours
- Ask how many hours per week the team actually has for marketing. If unknown, mark it `MISSING INFORMATION` rather than guessing.
- Give every activity an hours/week estimate and sum them. If the total exceeds the available hours, cut activities — never compress the estimates to make the math work.
- Give every channel a stop rule up front: "If fewer than N signups from this channel after 4 weeks at this level of effort, stop and move the hours elsewhere."
- Review weekly (15 minutes: what shipped, what the numbers say, what to stop) and monthly (which channel has earned more hours).
