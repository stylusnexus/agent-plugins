# April Dunford's five-component positioning method

April Dunford's positioning framework, explained here in this suite's own words, verified directly against April Dunford, "An Introduction to Positioning" — https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning

Positioning is context-setting: it decides what market you're claiming to win, and why, before a single word of copy gets written. There's no single "correct" positioning for a product — most products can be legitimately positioned more than one way, depending on which alternative and which segment you pick. The job is choosing the version that makes the product look strongest against a real alternative for a real buyer, not finding the one "true" positioning.

## The five components, in order

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would the customer use or do if this product didn't exist? This is broader than "named competitors" — it includes adjacent tools, manual workarounds, spreadsheets, and doing nothing. Naming the wrong alternative (a direct competitor when the real alternative is "do nothing") produces positioning that answers a question nobody's asking.
2. **Differentiated capabilities.** What do you have that those alternatives lack? List actual features and capabilities, not adjectives ("fast," "powerful") that any competitor could also claim.
3. **Value.** For each differentiated capability, what concrete outcome does it produce for the customer? A capability with no value attached is a spec sheet, not positioning.
4. **Target customer segment.** Of everyone who could plausibly use the product, which narrow segment cares *most* about that value? Positioning for "everyone" dilutes every claim. A segment that would pick the product for a different reason than the one you're leading with is the wrong segment for this positioning.
5. **Market category.** What frame makes the value obvious to that segment without a long explanation? Sometimes this is an existing, well-understood category. Sometimes the honest category understates the value and a reframed one works better — but a reframe still has to make sense to the buyer on first contact, not just to the team that invented it.

## How to run it

Work top to bottom, but expect to loop: choosing a different alternative in step 1 usually changes which capabilities matter in step 2, which can shift the segment in step 4. Do a full pass, then check whether the ending category still matches the alternative you started from — if a buyer comparing the product to the named alternative wouldn't recognize the category you picked, go back to step 1.

Write the choice down explicitly at each step, including the alternatives and segments you considered and rejected. That record is what lets you revisit positioning later without re-litigating it from scratch — a new competitor or a new segment might change the answer, but you'll know exactly which of the five components moved.

## Choosing the alternative deliberately
The same product can honestly have more than one starting point in step 1 — a named competing tool, or an unaided manual process a buyer does instead — and each implies different differentiated capabilities and likely a different segment. Pick one deliberately per launch motion rather than blending several into one pitch; a worked example lives in the [positioning canvas](../templates/positioning-canvas.md) template, not here.

Revisit positioning whenever a competitor is named repeatedly by evaluators, a new segment shows up in signups unprompted, or a launch is coming — not on a fixed schedule.
