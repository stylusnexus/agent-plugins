# The stakeholder check

Positioning (or any marketing move) can be internally consistent and still be a bad idea if it only works for one party. Checking a decision against everyone it touches — not just whether it converts — has real grounding in both marketing's own professional ethics and general business theory.

## Marketing's own professional standard

The American Marketing Association's Statement of Ethics sets "do no harm" as a core ethical norm: "not only consciously avoiding harmful actions or omissions but also striving to benefit all stakeholders and society at large." The stated values behind this are honesty, responsibility, equity, transparency, and citizenship. Source: American Marketing Association, "AMA Statement of Ethics" — https://www.ama.org/ama-statement-of-ethics/

## The general business case for checking beyond the shareholder

Business scholar R. Edward Freeman's stakeholder theory (1984) argues a firm should create value for everyone with a real stake in it — customers, employees, suppliers, and the community — not just whoever owns the equity. Applied to a single marketing decision, the same logic scales down: a positioning claim, a growth tactic, or an outreach approach has a customer-facing effect, a business effect, and an effect on the wider community or ecosystem it touches (competitors, the platform it's posted on, people who see the message but aren't the target) — and a decision that only survives scrutiny on one of those axes is worth a second look before it ships. Source: aggregated summaries of Freeman's *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach* — https://www.darden.virginia.edu/stakeholder-theory ; https://www.atlassian.com/work-management/project-collaboration/stakeholder-theory

## A real example of a company checking a decision this way in public

37signals has published its own reasoning for choosing profitability over outside investment, explicitly in terms of more than one stakeholder: "profitable companies are stable companies, and stable companies spell reliability for customers," and staying profitable is what let them "go in any direction you want and take as much time as you need" without answering to investors — a business decision defended by its effect on customers (reliability) and on the business's own independence, not by growth metrics alone. Source: 37signals, "Why We Choose Profit" — https://37signals.com/why-we-choose-profit

## Applying the check

For a positioning choice, or any marketing move: name what it does for the customer (is the claim true and useful to them, not just persuasive), what it does for the business (does it actually support a sustainable model, not just a short-term metric), and what it does to the wider community it touches (a competitor named unfairly, a platform's norms ignored, a claim that misleads people outside the target). A move that only checks out on the business axis, or only on the customer axis, is incomplete — say which axis is unresolved rather than shipping it anyway.
