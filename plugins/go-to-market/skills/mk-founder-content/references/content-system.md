# A weekly content system a small team can keep up

The goal: do one piece of real thinking, publish it once in full, then reuse it across several shorter posts, at a pace the team can hold for eight weeks.

**What's sourced and what isn't.** The numbers and the reuse principle cite the sources at the end. The steps themselves are this suite's own workflow reasoning, labeled INFERENCE where it matters.

## 1. Set the hours before picking the posts
Start with the hours a week the team can actually give, not with a posting target. For scale: Orbit Media's own annual survey of content marketers — its own respondent pool, not an independent study — reports that a blog post of about 1,300 words took an average of 3 hours 20 minutes to write in its most recent wave ([Orbit Media, 1,042 respondents, self-reported survey data](https://www.orbitmedia.com/blog/blogging-statistics/)). Treat that as a rough planning input, not a guarantee for any one team. With four spare hours a week, a founder can afford about one long piece plus light reuse. With two, make it one long piece a month.

## 2. Make one source piece, kept apart from its formats
Pick one substantial thing per cycle: a teardown of a decision, a how-to, a lesson from customer conversations, a set of real numbers. Publish it in full first on a space you own (blog, docs, newsletter).

The reuse idea comes from publishing systems. NPR's "Create Once, Publish Everywhere" approach stored content cleanly, apart from how any one page or app displayed it, so the same material could appear on many surfaces (EVIDENCE: [Jacobson, 2009](https://cybercemetery.unt.edu/oilspill/20130121135722mp_/http://blog.programmableweb.com/2009/10/13/cope-create-once-publish-everywhere)). For a founder, that means writing the substance once, free of any one platform's formatting, so each short post starts from it instead of a blank page (INFERENCE: this borrows the principle, not the newsroom's software).

## 3. Cut the week from the source piece
List the separate ideas in the source piece and turn each into a short post with a single point:
- **The finding:** the one claim, stated plainly, with its evidence.
- **The how:** one step a reader can try today.
- **The why:** the trade-off behind a decision, including what you gave up.
- **The proof:** a real, cited number or before/after. No number, no post.
- **The question:** end on a real question whose likely answers could feed the next source piece or short post — not a rhetorical close. Ask where the audience's experience differs, and treat the replies as a live input for what to write next.

Each post should stand alone and link back where allowed. Mix angles by what the audience responds to, not a quota (INFERENCE).

## 4. Choose a pace you can keep
Buffer, a social-scheduling vendor, published its own analysis of over 100,000 of its users' accounts: accounts that posted in 20 or more of 26 weeks got about five times the engagement per post of accounts that posted in four weeks or fewer ([Buffer, 2025, vendor's own user-data analysis](https://buffer.com/resources/consistent-posting-study/)). That's Buffer's own data about its own customer base, and it's a correlation — the study doesn't show that regular posting caused the lift, and it isn't independent evidence about posting in general. Buffer's separate frequency guide suggests 2–5 LinkedIn posts a week, but adds that for small businesses "the 'right' number is the number you can stick to" ([Buffer, 2026](https://buffer.com/resources/social-media-frequency-guide/)). Pick the lowest number that fits step 1's budget and can last eight weeks — the "right number" framing above is Buffer's own point, worth keeping regardless of the correlational data.

## 5. Batch, review, then ask
Draft the week's posts in one sitting right after the source piece, while it's fresh (INFERENCE: this saves rebuilding the context five times). Check it for unsourced claims and unapproved names, then show the owner. A plan is not permission. Ask before scheduling anything.
