# How feeds actually decide what to show

Every major feed platform publishes at least some real detail about its own ranking system, but none describe the same thing — each states its own signals, in its own terms. Treat what follows as what each platform itself says, not one universal model; don't blend platforms together when deciding how to post. This is a snapshot from one research pass — re-verify against the live page before relying on it, per [shared principles](../../marketing-lead/references/principles.md).

## X (Twitter)
X's own open-source recommendation code describes the For You timeline as built from two candidate sources: in-network posts (from accounts you follow, roughly half of candidates) and out-of-network posts (surfaced through other retrieval methods). Candidates then pass through a two-tier ranking step — a lighter model at initial retrieval, then a heavier neural-network ranker — before a `visibility-filters` stage removes content for legal or quality reasons and applies "hard-filtering, visible product treatments, and coarse-grained downranking." (github.com/twitter/the-algorithm — https://github.com/twitter/the-algorithm)

## LinkedIn
LinkedIn's engineering blog describes a first-pass candidate-generation layer that shortlists updates, followed by models that predict the probability a member acts on each candidate, its expected downstream engagement if reshared, and its expected value to the poster. The same post explains why LinkedIn also uses **dwell time** (time an update is visible on screen, and time spent after a click) as a ranking signal: clicks and reactions are "rare, especially for passive consumers" and "noisy indicators," while dwell time is "always measurable" and gives "a real-valued measure of engagement." (LinkedIn Engineering, "Understanding dwell time to improve LinkedIn feed ranking" — https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/understanding-feed-dwell-time)

## Meta (Facebook Feed)
Meta's Transparency Center describes Facebook Feed as a mix of "connected content" (friends, followed Pages, joined Groups) and "recommended content" (accounts you don't follow), ranked through four stated steps: **Inventory** (gathering eligible posts), **Signals** (reading "thousands of signals," explicit like likes/comments and implicit like time spent), **Predictions** (over 100 models predicting things like how likely you are to comment), and **Ranking** (a relevance score orders posts, aiming for "a balanced mix of content types"). (Meta Transparency Center, "Our Approach to Facebook Feed Ranking" — https://transparency.meta.com/features/ranking-and-content/)

## YouTube
YouTube's "How YouTube Works" site states recommendations are meant to "anticipate and meet a user's needs to drive value through relevant and satisfying viewing experiences," drawing on watch history, likes/dislikes, subscriptions, and satisfaction-survey feedback, plus channel reputation and current video topics; YouTube also says it uses external evaluators to judge how an average viewer might perceive content. (YouTube, "How YouTube Works — Recommendations" — https://www.youtube.com/howyoutubeworks/product-features/recommendations/)

## Applying it
Ask platform-specific questions, not one universal one: on LinkedIn, does this post give someone a reason to stop and read, not just glance and scroll (dwell time is a named signal there)? On X, is this written to earn a reply or repost from people who don't already follow the account, since out-of-network reach runs through a separate candidate path? On Facebook, would a stranger's implicit signals (time spent, not just a reaction) say this was worth their attention? On YouTube, does the video satisfy what its title and thumbnail promise, since dissatisfaction feeds back into future recommendations? None of this is a guaranteed mechanism — only what each platform states about itself, right now.
