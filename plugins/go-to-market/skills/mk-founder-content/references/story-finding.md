# Finding and telling a true story

A founder-post claim lands harder with a real story behind it than with an adjective in front of it. This file is about finding a story that's actually true and giving it a shape that reads as a story, not a list of claims with an anecdote glued on the front.

## Finding it: look for a real before/after
Nancy Duarte's research into what makes talks and presentations resonate identifies a recurring pattern: the strongest ones keep contrasting "what is" — the real, current state of things — against "what could be," an envisioned change, building tension until the talk resolves it. (Source: Nancy Duarte, "The Secret Structure of Great Talks," TEDxEast, November 2011 — https://www.ted.com/talks/nancy_duarte_the_secret_structure_of_great_talks — accessed 2026-09-24.) The same pattern is the fastest way to find a real founder story: instead of "what should I write about," ask "where did something real actually change" — a customer's specific problem before it was fixed, a mistake the team made and what changed after, a moment the product's direction shifted because of something real. If there's no real "before" that's different from a real "now," there's no story yet — just a claim looking for one.

## Structuring it: a real, simple spine
Once there's a real before/after, the story spine created by improviser Kenn Adams gives a simple, well-tested shape for putting it in order. Adams built it in the late 1980s teaching creative writing, as a tool for students to outline how a story moves: a fixed set of sentence openers — "Once upon a time... Every day... But one day... Because of that... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally... And ever since then" — with the real details of the actual story filled in after each. (Source: Kenn Adams, described in "Meet the creator of the Story Spine, an 8-sentence tool to create and analyze stories" — https://www.cfpublic.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories — published 2026-06-23, accessed 2026-09-24.) The spine later reached Pixar through improv teacher Rebecca Stockley, who had learned it from Adams; that's why a version of the same shape shows up, expanded, in feature films. A short founder post rarely needs all eight beats spelled out; the value is confirming the real story has a before, a specific turning event, and a real after — not reciting the openers themselves in the post.

## What a real one looks like
The same shape at company-blog scale: GitLab announced on 2023-01-17 that only pre-listed contacts could open a support ticket starting 2023-02-01, replacing a system where anyone could file one but only paid-org users got an SLA-bound reply — "every named support contact will receive support without the need for additional clarification." A real, dated before; a real, dated change; a stated reason — not a smoothed-over composite. (Source: GitLab, "GitLab Support requires contacts to be listed to open Support tickets" — https://about.gitlab.com/blog/gitlab-support-requires-contacts-to-be-listed-to-open-support-tickets/ — published 2023-01-17, accessed 2026-09-24. The page carries no Creative Commons notice; it's ordinary © GitLab content, quoted briefly here for commentary, not reproduced.)

## Keeping it true
Every beat has to be checkable: an actual message, an actual date, an actual number, or a direct account from the person it happened to. Anything invented to make the shape fit better — a detail smoothed over, a timeline compressed in a way that changes what happened, a quote nobody said — comes back out, even if the story reads better with it in. A story that's slightly less dramatic and entirely true is worth more than a better-shaped one that isn't.
