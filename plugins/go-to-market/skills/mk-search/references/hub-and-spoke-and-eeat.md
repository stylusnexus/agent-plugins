# Hub structure and trust signals

Two separate things make a topic findable and credible: organizing related pages so neither search engines nor readers have to guess how they connect, and giving both of them real reasons to trust what's on the page. Both are public, well-documented practices, worth grounding a workflow in directly rather than reinventing.

## Organizing a topic: a hub page and its supporting pages
HubSpot, which popularized this way of organizing content, describes the pattern in its own terms: one broad page — the hub — that gives a comprehensive overview of a topic, and a set of narrower supporting pages, each answering one specific question the hub is "too broad to address." The supporting pages link back to the hub with descriptive link text, and the hub links out to the supporting pages that cover its subtopics in depth. HubSpot's own explanation of why this helps: for a search engine, "the internal links tell crawlers that the content shares a semantic relationship, which strengthens topical authority over time"; for a reader, the hub page "gives a broad overview, and from there, a reader can drop into the specific subtopic they came for," instead of going back to search for every follow-up question. (Source: HubSpot, "Topic clusters: The next evolution of SEO," https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/topic-clusters-seo, accessed 2026-09-24, updated 2026-06-17.)

Practically: before writing a new page, check whether it's actually the hub for its topic (nothing broader exists yet) or a supporting page under one that already does. A supporting page with no hub to link back to, and a hub with no supporting pages linking in, both lose most of the benefit — the value is in the two-way linking, not in either page alone.

## Trust signals: what Google actually asks
Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines use E-E-A-T — Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust — as the framework its human raters apply when judging content quality, and state plainly that "the most important member at the center of the E-E-A-T family is Trust." Google added the first "Experience" in a December 2022 update to what had been "E-A-T," specifically to credit content showing "actual use of a product" or genuine first-hand experience with a topic, not credentials alone. (Sources: Google, Search Quality Rater Guidelines, §3.4 "Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trust (E-E-A-T)," https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf, edition dated 2025-09-11, accessed 2026-09-24; Google, "Our latest update to the quality rater guidelines: E-A-T gets an extra E for Experience," https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2022/12/google-raters-guidelines-e-e-a-t, published 2022-12, accessed 2026-09-24.) The same guidelines instruct raters to check "who is responsible for the website and who created the content on the page" and to look for "About Us, Contact Information, and Customer Service Information" as part of that trust assessment — the amount of detail expected scales with the page (an online store needs more than a personal blog, and user-generated content may reasonably use an alias). (Source: same PDF, §§2.5.2–2.5.3.) Separately, Google's helpful-content page asks directly "Is it self-evident to your visitors who authored your content?" and "Do pages carry a byline, where one might be expected?" and states "of these aspects, trust is most important." (Source: Google, "Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content," https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content, accessed 2026-09-24.)

What that looks like on an actual page, for a small team without institutional credentials to lean on: say who wrote it and why they're a reasonable person to have written it (a byline, not just a company name); show real, checkable evidence of having actually done the thing being described, rather than only describing it in the abstract; link out to real sources for any claim that isn't the writer's own first-hand account; and keep visible contact or about information, scaled to what the page actually needs. None of this is about satisfying a rubric — it's the same thing a skeptical human reader checks for before deciding whether to act on what a page says.
