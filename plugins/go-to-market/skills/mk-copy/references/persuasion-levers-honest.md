# Persuasion levers, filtered through honest use

Robert Cialdini's widely-cited principles of persuasion name a set of triggers that make people more likely to say yes. His own institute currently lists **seven**: reciprocity, scarcity, authority, consistency, liking, social proof, and unity (added after the earlier six-principle formulation, per the page's own footnote: "This was created before the addition of the 7th Principle of Unity"). Source: Influence at Work, "Dr. Robert Cialdini's Seven Principles of Persuasion" — https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/, accessed 2026-09-24. This suite covers the original six below; unity (shared identity between persuader and audience) isn't separately broken out. None of these are inherently dishonest — they describe how people actually decide. They become manipulation only when the trigger is faked. The filter this skill applies to every one of them: **use a lever only when it is true; if a tactic only works when the audience doesn't know what's happening, don't use it.**

## The levers, filtered

- **Scarcity** — usable only when the limit is real (real capacity, a real deadline). Never usable as invented urgency on something with no actual constraint. A skeptical, technical audience is especially good at spotting manufactured urgency, and detecting it costs more trust than the lever gains.
- **Social proof** — usable only with real testimonials, real numbers, real reviews. Never a "trusted by thousands" line with no names or numbers behind it.
- **Authority / credibility** — usable only with real, checkable claims: an actual audit, an actual fixed defect, an actual shipped feature. For a technical audience this is one of the stronger honest levers available, but only when backed by a real result — never a vague "enterprise-grade" claim.
- **Reciprocity (give before you ask)** — usable and honest: a genuinely useful free tool, template, or piece of writing, given with no strings attached, earns goodwill on its own. It stops being honest the moment the "free" thing is a disguised trap to extract something bigger later.
- **Commitment and consistency (small ask first)** — usable when every ask along the way is honestly framed as what it is. A newsletter signup that's genuinely just a newsletter signup, followed later by a real invite to something bigger, is fine. A small ask deliberately designed to manipulate someone into a bigger one they wouldn't otherwise choose is not.
- **Letting people try before they commit** — related to what behavioral economics calls the endowment effect: "people are more likely to retain an object they own than acquire that same object when they do not own it," and Wikipedia's marketing-application section notes free trials work partly because they "give consumers a sense of ownership" that makes them "more reluctant to part with the service when the trial ends." Source: Wikipedia, "Endowment effect" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endowment_effect, accessed 2026-09-24. Usable and honest here: real hands-on use before asking for a purchase decision isn't manipulation, it's giving someone real information to decide with — the line is the same one as everywhere else in this file, don't design the trial to trap someone into a purchase they haven't actually evaluated.
- **Leaning on existing beliefs** — related to confirmation bias, "the tendency to search for, interpret, favor and recall information in a way that confirms or supports one's prior beliefs." Source: Wikipedia, "Confirmation bias" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias, accessed 2026-09-24. The riskiest lever here. Deliberately shaping copy to flatter what a reader already believes, in order to avoid an honest but less comfortable truth, crosses into the manipulation this filter rules out. If the honest message happens to agree with what the reader already believes, that's fine — engineering it that way on purpose is not.

## Structural choices that need no filtering
Where you put the strongest point in a piece of copy relates to the serial-position effect: in free recall, "people tend to begin recall with the end of the list" (the recency effect) and "the first few items are recalled more frequently than the middle items" (the primacy effect). Source: Wikipedia, "Serial-position effect" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial-position_effect, accessed 2026-09-24. Matching a message to how aware the reader already is of the problem relates to the hierarchy-of-effects model, which describes stages (awareness, knowledge, liking, among others) a buyer moves through before purchase — someone who's never heard of the problem needs a different opening than someone already at the "knowledge" or "liking" stage. Source: Corporate Finance Institute, "Hierarchy of Effects" — https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/management/hierarchy-of-effects/, accessed 2026-09-24. Both are sequencing choices, not truth claims — they don't need the honesty filter the levers above do. The honesty check still applies to whatever content fills that sequence.

## Document the choice, don't just apply it
When a draft ships, name which levers above it actually uses and why the fact behind each is true and checkable right now — and name any lever considered and left out (most often because the fact isn't real yet: no real urgency, no real testimonial on hand). This note travels with the draft, not buried in this reference file.

## Net guidance
Reciprocity, letting people try first, and the structural sequencing choices are close to risk-free. Social proof, authority, and commitment/consistency are usable but conditional — the fact behind them has to be true and checkable, every time, for this specific product, right now. Scarcity and belief-flattering framing are the two to be most careful with, because a technical or hobbyist audience is unusually good at detecting manufactured urgency or engineered flattery. The honesty filter itself is this suite's own rule — see [shared principles](../../marketing-lead/references/principles.md).
