# Finding the core segment before building lifecycle email

Grounded in Sean Ellis's product-market-fit survey method (popularized publicly through Rahul Vohra's account of building it at Superhuman — Source: First Round Review, "How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product-Market Fit," https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/) and Patrick McKenzie's public writing on email as a product lever (Source: "You Should Probably Send More Email Than You Do," https://www.kalzumeus.com/2012/05/31/can-i-get-your-email/).

## The survey
Ask active users — or an early-access or waitlist cohort that has actually used the product, never one that hasn't — this question: **"How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?"** Offer three answers: very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed. Sean Ellis's benchmark is that ≥40% answering "very disappointed" suggests real product-market fit for that segment. A cohort that hasn't used the product yet isn't eligible for this survey at all — send them a welcome or confirmation email and, at most, one question about what they hope to do with the product, and wait for real usage before asking the PMF question.

## What to do with the answers
1. Segment respondents into rough personas — for a scheduling tool, that might be solo freelancers versus small agency teams — and find which persona over-indexes on "very disappointed." That persona is who the product should be built for first.
2. Read open-ended feedback only from the "very disappointed" and "somewhat disappointed" groups whose stated primary benefit actually matches what the product is strong at. Feedback from people who wouldn't care if the product vanished isn't a design brief.
3. Split effort between the two: deepen what the very-disappointed segment already loves, and fix the specific blockers holding back the somewhat-disappointed group. Neither gets skipped.
4. Repeat the survey on a rolling basis and track the percent very-disappointed as a standing number, not a one-time exercise.

## Why this comes before the email sequence
A generic onboarding sequence tries to walk everyone toward some vague "aha" moment. This survey identifies which narrow group is already sold, and what specifically sold them — so the onboarding sequence's job becomes "get more people to that specific experience, fast," a concrete design target instead of a guess.

## Behavior over schedule (this suite's own reasoning)
Once the segment and its "aha" moment are known, aim revenue asks at people who are already loyal, not brand-new signups, and trigger those asks on what a person did or didn't do rather than a fixed calendar. This is the suite's own reasoning, not McKenzie's — his cited post actually argues the opposite for list email generally: he describes emailing his own list "approximately" monthly and says "every time I blast out an email I get a few hundred bucks," and the Blue Nile example he gives is a case of volume working, not behavior-triggering — he says the retailer "sent me approximately 45 newsletters" after he bought an engagement ring, and when he later needed wedding rings, "they were sitting at the top of my inbox. Guess who got the order?" That's evidence for frequent, scheduled email, cited here only for the general point that lifecycle/drip email is a real lever — not for the behavior-over-schedule claim above.

## When there's no answer yet
If a product has never run this survey, say so plainly rather than guessing at what email 1 should promise. Vohra's own guidance: "you start to get directionally correct results around 40 respondents, which is much less than most people think" (First Round Review, same source as above). Treat anything smaller as informal only — a rough read worth having, but not a reliable check against the 40% benchmark, which needs the fuller sample to trust.
