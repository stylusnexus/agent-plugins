# Brand voice and the email feedback loop

Lifecycle email is almost never one person's voice — it's the product's. This reference covers how to keep it that way while drafting, and what's actually safe to conclude once real sends come back.

## Draft from the product's own words, not a list of adjectives

"Friendly but professional" tells a drafting pass nothing usable — everyone reads those two words differently. What works instead is pulling real sentences the product has already said in public: the README's opening line, the actual copy on the pricing page, a founder's own phrasing from a changelog or post. Draft the email by writing toward that register, then check the draft against the real examples side by side — does it sound like the same product wrote both, or does the email suddenly sound like a different, more generic company showed up. If the product doesn't have enough existing copy to draft from yet, say so and default to the plainest, most direct version of the sentence rather than inventing a "brand voice" from nothing.

Before sending, run the draft through a review for generic AI phrasing and a review for unsupported claims — anything that reads as a promise ("saves you hours," "the best way to") needs a fact behind it or has to go.

## Measure what the send can actually tell you

Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, live since iOS 15, has Apple Mail pre-load and cache a message's images — including the open-tracking pixel — by routing it through an Apple-hosted proxy, whether or not the recipient ever opens it (Source: Postmark, "Open Tracking and Apple Mail (Mail Privacy Protection)," https://postmarkapp.com/support/article/1257-open-tracking-and-apple-mail). Postmark's blog is direct about what that does to the number: "pixel-based email tracking has never been a perfect science, and Apple's changes only made an imprecise metric even more unreliable" (Source: Postmark, "How Apple's Mail Privacy Changes Affect Email Open Tracking," https://postmarkapp.com/blog/how-apples-mail-privacy-changes-affect-email-open-tracking). Postmark's own support article points senders toward sturdier signals instead: "look for ways to engage your recipients as a willing participant, such as asking for feedback on your content or letting them reply to your messages directly," rather than reading opens as ground truth.

Practically: treat open rate as a rough signal at best, not a metric to optimize a subject line against. Clicks, conversions, replies, and unsubscribes still mean what they say — a click requires an actual person to act, and an unsubscribe requires an actual person to decide they're done. Build the read on those, and note open rate (if it's tracked at all) as context, not as the headline number in a readout.

## Close the loop without over-fitting to one send

After a real send, read the numbers together rather than reacting to any single one in isolation — a low click rate and a high unsubscribe rate on the same email usually point to different problems, one about relevance, one about frequency or expectations. Where a reply raises a genuine objection ("I thought this was free," "why am I getting this"), that's real information about what the copy assumed and got wrong — worth changing in the next draft, and worth flagging to whoever owns positioning if the objection is about the product itself rather than the email.

One send is one data point. Don't rewrite the whole sequence off a single email's numbers; wait for a pattern across two or three before concluding the voice, not just that particular subject line, needs to change.
