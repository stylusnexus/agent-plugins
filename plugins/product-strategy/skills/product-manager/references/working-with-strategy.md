# Working with strategy

Treat strategy as a set of choices that can be acted on now and revised through learning. Avoid both a one-off document ceremony and endless indecision.

## Can one workshop create it?
A prepared workshop can synthesize evidence, surface disagreement, compare choices, and produce an initial strategy or make an informed decision. It cannot create missing customer or economic evidence through discussion. Label the result with its evidence status. Do not claim that a workshop validates a strategy or that no useful strategic decision is possible in one meeting.

Before a workshop: read existing decisions and research, identify the decision to make, invite relevant perspectives, and prepare contrasting options. During: examine evidence, map choices, expose trade-offs, and choose or identify the precise uncertainty. After: assign tests and owners, record decisions, and schedule a review based on learning.

## Can strategy be done?
A strategy version can be accepted, communicated, and ready to guide work. It is not eternally validated. Review at a cadence proportionate to uncertainty and when specific triggers occur: assumptions fail, user needs change, a rival responds, economics shift, a capability fails to materialize, or relevant technology changes. Avoid reacting to every anecdote by replacing the strategy. Record whether new evidence changes a tactic, an objective, or a strategic choice, and explain why.

## Who should be involved?
Include the accountable owner, product/design/engineering perspectives, and relevant business/operational perspectives. Bring customer evidence into decisions without asking customers to design the strategy. Collaborate without requiring unanimous agreement. Document disagreement, decision ownership, and the reasoning people need to act autonomously.

For a solo builder, combine roles pragmatically. AI specialists can examine different angles but are not independent customer evidence and do not substitute for real-world research. Never fabricate stakeholder agreement or imply an agent has interviewed customers.

## Discover and revise
Structure iterative strategy work on the UK Design Council's Double Diamond, first shared publicly in 2004 per the Design Council's own history of the model. This plugin frames the diamonds as a problem space (Discover, Define) and a solution space (Develop, Deliver); the Design Council's own words, from team member Anna White on its history page, are "designing the right thing" for the first half and "designing the thing right" for the second (<https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/>, <https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/history-of-the-double-diamond/>):
1. **Discover:** understand the problem rather than assume it — interviews, observation, support signal, analytics, relevant public data, and alternatives. Choose sources suited to the decision.
2. **Define:** reframe the challenge from what discovery surfaced — jobs, needs, segments, importance/satisfaction, constraints, competitive context, and a candidate value proposition. Preserve uncertainty about market size or economics.
3. **Develop:** generate different answers to the now-defined problem — compare product concepts, customer experience, channels, pricing, positioning, and messaging, including no-build options.
4. **Deliver:** test at small scale — demand/engagement, value, usability, viability, feasibility, and ethical consequences with appropriate evidence — discarding what doesn't hold up and refining what does.
5. **Feed back:** update the problem, segment, value proposition, or broader choices as warranted. A completed pass supports bounded conclusions; it does not validate all of a strategy forever.

Do not confuse a large framework diagram with a mandatory sequence. Start where uncertainty is consequential, make useful decisions, and loop back to Discover when evidence warrants it.
