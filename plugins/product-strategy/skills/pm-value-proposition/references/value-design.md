# Developing the value proposition

This is the "how" behind the [value proposition working sheet](../templates/value-proposition.md) — read that first; this file explains the moves it asks for and where the value proposition's job ends.

## Start from a job, not a feature

Jobs to Be Done theory (Clayton Christensen, with Bob Moesta) argues that people don't buy products, they "hire" them to make progress on a job — and the real competition is whatever else could do that job, not the nearest lookalike product. The theory's own worked example: morning milkshake buyers were competing against bagels and bananas, not other milkshakes, because the shake was hired "to satiate hunger, make their drive more interesting, and easily hold food in the car" — a different job from the afternoon shake, bought as "a treat for their kids." ([Christensen Institute, "Jobs to Be Done Theory."](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)) A value proposition written against a feature category ("a better milkshake") targets the wrong competitive set; one written against the job doesn't.

Strategyn's Outcome-Driven Innovation, credited in the worksheet, makes this operational: break the job into steps, then write a desired-outcome statement — one specific, measurable way the job executor judges whether a step is going well — for each. The worksheet's sections 2 and 3 do this directly; use them rather than re-deriving it here.

## Design moves once you have underserved outcomes

The worksheet's section 3 surfaces which desired outcomes are important and poorly satisfied today — that's where a value proposition has room to matter. Two moves follow, and it's worth keeping them apart:

- **The outcome** — what changes for the job executor, stated in their terms and checkable against the desired-outcome statement, not in terms of what the product contains.
- **The mechanism** — how the offering actually produces that change. Section 5 asks for this separately on purpose: an unstated mechanism is a claim with nothing behind it, and an outcome restated as a mechanism ("we have AI-powered search" describes a capability, not a change in the job executor's life) quietly substitutes the capability for the benefit it's supposed to produce.

Test a candidate statement by asking whether outcome and mechanism could be swapped without anyone noticing. If they can, one of the two hasn't actually been specified yet.

## Where this stops

April Dunford draws a boundary worth keeping: "positioning defines what market you intend to win and why you deserve to win it" — and is explicit that positioning is not messaging, a tagline, or brand narrative, even though those depend on it. ([April Dunford, "An Introduction to Positioning,"](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning) 2020-05-04.) A value proposition, in this worksheet's sense, sits one layer below that: the evidence-backed claim that a specific offering changes a specific outcome for a specific job executor, better than today's alternatives. Positioning and messaging depend on getting this right first and are separate, out-of-scope work. Treat a fluent statement as a thinking aid to sharpen the claim, not proof the claim is correct — polished wording doesn't substitute for the evidence status recorded next to it.

## Evidence status

Every underserved outcome, mechanism, and the statement built from them carries an evidence status — **evidence-backed**, **hypothesis**, or **accepted** — per [shared principles](../../product-manager/references/principles.md). A value proposition built entirely from hypotheses is still worth writing down; it just isn't ready to be treated as a decision.

## A different public framework, for comparison

Strategyzer's Value Proposition Canvas is the better-known tool here, asking a related but differently shaped question: map the job executor's jobs, pains, and gains against the offering's pain relievers and gain creators — "map what customers are trying to get done, what gets in their way, and what they want to achieve. Then design an offer that fits their needs." ([Strategyzer, "The Value Proposition Canvas."](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-value-proposition-canvas)) This worksheet isn't that canvas — it runs job steps through desired-outcome statements, not pains/gains — but either can supply the underserved-outcome evidence the moves above need.
