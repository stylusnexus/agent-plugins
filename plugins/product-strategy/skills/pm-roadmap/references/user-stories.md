# User stories and how to split them

User stories in the "As a [user], I want [action], so that [outcome]" shape grew out of Extreme Programming. Per Mike Cohn, the template itself "originated with agile coach Rachel Davies at a UK company, Connextra, in the early 2000s," and Cohn's own writing then helped popularize it for backlog work ([Mountain Goat Software, "User Story Template: What It Is and Why It Works So Well"](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/why-the-three-part-user-story-template-works-so-well)). INVEST (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable) as a review checklist for a story comes from Bill Wake: [INVEST in Good Stories, and SMART Tasks](https://xp123.com/invest-in-good-stories-and-smart-tasks/) at xp123.com, 2003-08-17. The job-story alternative ("When [situation], I want [motivation], so I can [outcome]") was developed at Intercom by Paul Adams and named by Alan Klement ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/accidentally-invented-job-stories/)). This reference applies those ideas operationally; it is not a reproduction of any single course or article.

## Before writing the story
Ground it first: who is this for, what evidence says the problem is real, which strategic objective it serves, and what outcome would count as success. A story is a *placeholder for a conversation*, not proof that the idea has been validated — writing it down doesn't make it true. Use the classic user-story sentence when there's a clear actor and action; switch to a job-story framing when the triggering situation matters more than the persona label. Don't force a fictional "user" onto work that isn't user-facing — a bug fix, an infrastructure change, or a research spike should be named for what it is, not dressed up as a persona's wish.

## Card, Conversation, Confirmation
Ron Jeffries' original three-part model still holds ([XP123, "INVEST in Good Stories, and SMART Tasks,"](https://xp123.com/invest-in-good-stories-and-smart-tasks/) Bill Wake, 2003-08-17: "Ron Jeffries points out that stories in XP have three components: Cards..., Conversation..., and Confirmation..."): the written story is a short *card* — just enough to remember what was agreed to have a conversation about. The *conversation* is where the real detail lives: rationale, alternatives considered, constraints, and dependencies, worked through with the people who'll build and use it. The *confirmation* is the observable acceptance criteria the team agrees define "done." Keep room for the story to change as people learn more, but keep the acceptance criteria explicit enough that "done" isn't a matter of opinion. Passing the acceptance tests confirms the software does what was specified — it does not confirm the customer outcome actually happened; that needs its own follow-up measurement.

## Using INVEST as a prompt, not a gate
Run each story through Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, and Testable as a set of questions, not a pass/fail checklist to force compliance on. A story with a real dependency on another team's work is still worth writing — name the dependency rather than pretending it doesn't exist just to claim independence. Size a slice to be genuinely useful and testable; there's no universal "right" story size or epic duration that applies across every team and domain.

## Splitting a story to learn faster
Common ways to split a larger piece of work into smaller, still-valuable slices: by core path versus optional/edge-case path, by workflow step, by a specific business rule variant, by a section of the interface, by data type or format, by supported platform, or by audience segment. These patterns are widely taught in agile splitting guides (Richard Lawrence's story-splitting work is a well-known source); prefer whichever split still delivers something a user could actually use end-to-end. When a piece of the work is a pure technical enabler with no direct user value on its own, label it honestly as an enabler and tie it explicitly to the user-facing outcome it unlocks — don't let "technical enabler" become an excuse for months of ungrounded groundwork.

A smaller slice is not an excuse to drop what the full feature needed: authorization checks, data integrity, accessibility, and basic error handling for whatever scope did ship. A "happy path only" split is fine for sequencing; it is not a license to ship something that mishandles the states it doesn't yet support — those states should fail safely or be explicitly out of scope for this release, not silently broken.

## What a story record should carry
A short title; the story or job statement with its context; a link to the outcome it's meant to move and the evidence behind it; scope and explicit non-goals; the relevant flow or design reference; acceptance criteria stated as observable conditions (kept separate from the detailed test procedures that verify them); known dependencies; and how success will be measured. Include the meaningful empty, error, and permission-denied cases when they matter for this slice.

## A short worked example
**Title:** Export a shortlist
**Story:** As someone recommending a few items to a friend, I want to export just my shortlist, so that I can share it without asking them to create an account.
**Outcome hypothesis:** a recipient who has never used the product can understand the recommendations without extra explanation.
**Acceptance criteria:** only the selected items appear; their order and titles are preserved; any private notes are excluded from the export; an empty shortlist produces a clear message instead of a blank file; a failed export leaves the original selection intact and offers a retry. The exact export format and which fields are included are open decisions to settle with the user, not assumptions to bake in silently.

## Working with the team
Refine stories collaboratively, close to when the work will actually start, rather than trying to fully spec a backlog months out. When scope changes materially, record it and revisit affected estimates and dependencies — don't quietly rewrite a commitment someone already agreed to. What to do with stale or distant ideas (archive, delete, defer) should follow the project's own conventions, not an arbitrary universal expiry rule. Product and engineering should work out the consequences of a choice together; engineering owns how it gets built. Exact role names and titles vary by organization and aren't load-bearing here.

Teaching a user through one story and one splitting decision, using their actual product, is more useful than a generic lecture — then produce whatever artifact they asked for. Writing a story is not itself authorization to open a tracking issue or start building it.
