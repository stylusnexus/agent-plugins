# Is it a critical problem?

Use this when choosing which problem to build for, before comparing solutions. It adds to the interview guidance in [research-interviews](research-interviews.md); it does not replace it. The ideas are paraphrased from bootstrapped-SaaS practice (Arvid Kahl, *Zero to Sold*, 2020) and Eugene Schwartz's awareness levels; verify against the originals before quoting.

## Signals of a critical problem
A candidate problem is stronger the more of these hold, each backed by something a person said or did:
- It recurs often, not once a year.
- It costs time or money each time it happens.
- People cannot simply opt out of it.
- They already work around it: a spreadsheet, a document template, stitched-together tools, or hired help.
- Fixing it saves time, saves money, or makes money.

A problem with none of these is a nice-to-have. Say so, and don't let a polite "that would be useful" count as evidence.

## Rank by intensity
Place each problem on an importance-versus-urgency grid:
- Important and urgent: most intense. Best to solve, strongest willingness to pay.
- Important, not urgent: tedious. Good retention, slower to sell.
- Urgent, not important: pressing but price-sensitive.
- Neither: an annoyance. Expect high churn and high acquisition cost.

## Note how aware the person is
Record, per person, whether they are unaware of the problem, aware of it, aware solutions exist, aware of your product, or ready to buy. People who are aware of the problem but not of solutions are the richest source of problem statements. Unaware people need education first, which is expensive.

## Run it
1. For each conversation, write down the problem, how intense it is, why it exists, and the person's awareness level.
2. Merge lists across people, count repeats, and rank by intensity.
3. Validate the top problem with follow-up conversations with a random subset.
4. Record a call and wait a few days before analyzing it. Right after a call, first impressions and confirmation bias are strongest. Keep a short note of anything that looked like bias (flattery, pitching, someone with no stake) and don't act on that conversation.

## Limits
Small, hand-picked samples show a pattern, not market size. Say what the conversations cannot tell you.
