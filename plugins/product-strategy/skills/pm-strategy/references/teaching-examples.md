# Teaching strategy through worked examples

The three cases below are real, drawn from what each company or writer has published about itself for free — not invented companies. Each quote is short and linked back to its source; treat the source page as authoritative over this summary, and don't extend a claim past what the page itself says. A real example still isn't proof about your product: it shows a pattern, not a guarantee.

## How to use an example with a user
Pick the one case below that's structurally closest to the user's situation, walk through *one* lesson it illustrates, and stop there — don't narrate the whole case. Then ask how that lesson would actually apply in the user's product, and draft their real version together. Treat the exercise as optional scaffolding: if the user just wants their own work done, do that first and skip the walkthrough.

## Case A — a metric that could move in the wrong direction
**Real case: YouTube's watch-time OKR.** Around 2011, YouTube's Cristos Goodrow, then leading search and discovery, shifted the company's core metric from view count to total watch time, reasoning that longer watch time meant a more satisfied audience — a decision Goodrow later described in his own words: "we thought the amount of time someone spent watching a video was a better way to understand whether a viewer really enjoyed it" (Cristos Goodrow, ["You know what's cool? A billion hours"](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/you-know-whats-cool-billion-hours/), YouTube Official Blog, 2017-02-27). By 2012 this became a company-wide OKR, with the billion-hours goal itself credited to YouTube technical leader Shishir Mehrotra in John Doerr's account: "Reach 1 billion hours of watch time per day [by 2016]," with key results covering Search + Main App, Living Room, and a YouTube VR launch. YouTube hit the goal in October 2016. Sources: Jacques Corby-Tuech, ["OKR's, YouTube and the Danger of Unintended Consequences"](https://www.jacquescorbytuech.com/writing/okr-youtube-unintended-consequences) (published 2020-01-22, per the page's dateline; relaying the account from John Doerr's *Measure What Matters* and the source for the OKR wording and names above); whatmatters.com, John Doerr's own OKR site, corroborates the 2012 pivot from views to watch time in its [company-wide OKR examples](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/company-wide-okr-ideas-examples).
- **Teaching question:** what customer behavior would actually make watch time go up, and is that behavior something the company wants more of?
- **Critical check:** the same page ties the outcome directly to Goodhart's law, quoted on the page: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The recommendation engine that hit the watch-time target also optimized for whatever kept people watching longest — which, as reported in the piece's later sections (citing Bloomberg and other reporting on the same events), included extremist and conspiratorial content that was unusually effective at extending watch sessions. A single number can't distinguish "people found what they needed" from "the algorithm found what keeps people watching."
- **Where it can go wrong:** optimizing hard for one proxy metric, with no paired guardrail metric for the thing the proxy was supposed to stand in for (audience wellbeing, content quality), can hit the number while the thing it was meant to represent moves the opposite way — and the team may not find out until well after the target is declared a win.

## Case B — a trade-off the company has to actually make, not just claim
**Real case: Basecamp's Shape Up calendar feature.** In *Shape Up* (Basecamp/37signals, Ryan Singer, published 2019, free at [basecamp.com/shapeup](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.1-chapter-02)), Basecamp explains why it didn't rebuild a full calendar when redesigning the product: "Past versions of Basecamp had calendars, and only about 10% of customers used them. That's why we didn't have the appetite for spending six months on a calendar. On the other hand, if we could do something to satisfy those customers who were writing us in one six week cycle, we were open to doing that." Working inside that fixed six-week "appetite," the team built a deliberately reduced view — a read-only, two-month grid they called the "Dot Grid" — instead of a full-featured calendar.
- **Teaching question:** what does the declined scope (drag-and-drop, multi-day events, color coding, monthly/weekly/daily views — all named on the same page as things they explicitly left out) cost the 10% of customers who wanted it, and did the company say so out loud?
- **Critical check:** the trade-off is named, not just implied — a specific usage number (10%), a specific declined scope (six months of full calendar features), and a specific fixed constraint (six weeks) that forced the choice. Ask the same of any claimed trade-off: is there a number behind "only a few customers," and is the boundary of what's declined actually written down anywhere, or just implied by omission?
- **Where it can go wrong:** without a stated appetite and a named declined scope, "we're prioritizing" quietly becomes "we're building a little of everything," and nobody can point to what got cut or why.

## Case C — coherent choices that reinforce each other, and the ones that don't
**Real case: GitLab's 2026 mission ("Act 2").** GitLab's public mission page states the company's current mission as "Unlock every team to ship trusted software at the speed of imagination," succeeding its earlier mission, "everyone can contribute." The same page lists five stated "architectural bets," including "Context as the moat" — "Enterprise AI bills are climbing as fast as adoption. What doesn't commoditize is the context the model gets to work with" — and "Governance in the core" — "Governance is what lets enterprises move fast in the agentic era... we're building identity, audit, policy, and deployment flexibility as core platform services... rather than a separate product layered on top." Source: ["GitLab Mission" — The GitLab Handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/mission/) (page states "Last modified May 13, 2026"; the handbook is openly licensed for reuse — quoted briefly here, not reproduced).
- **Teaching question:** do the stated bets point the same direction, or does one quietly undercut another?
- **Critical check:** the mission page states the bets are meant to reinforce each other — governance and context are both framed as reasons enterprises can trust the platform with agent-driven work, and both are described as built into the core platform rather than sold as an add-on ("rather than a separate product layered on top"). That's a claim of coherence the page itself makes; whether it holds up in GitLab's actual roadmap and pricing decisions is not something this page alone can verify — it's the company's own stated intent, not independently audited follow-through.
- **Where it can go wrong:** a mission page can list several bets that read as reinforcing on the page while the actual roadmap, hiring, and pricing decisions pull in different directions — coherence has to be checked against what the company actually ships, not just what it publishes.

## Turning critique into a habit
Apply the same questions to any example, real or hypothetical, including the ones above:
- Does a stated or implied *metric* actually track customer value, or could it move in the wrong direction while looking good? (Case A)
- Is a "trade-off" a deliberate, reasoned choice with a named loser and a stated number, a temporary gap the team intends to close, an unverified assumption, or just an external constraint dressed up as a decision? These require different responses. (Case B)
- Do the strategic choices in front of you actually reinforce each other in practice — not just in how they're described — the way Roger Martin's choice cascade (aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, management systems) argues they should? (Case C)
- When a company serves multiple segments, don't blend their value propositions into a single mushy statement — pick the segment relevant to the decision at hand and name any dependency between segments explicitly.

## Choosing how to represent the analysis
Pick the representation by what question needs answering, not by a fixed rule: a business-model or lean canvas when the economics and participants are still unclear, a strategy canvas (see `pm-canvas`) when several strategic choices need to be seen together, and a plain trade-off table when the discussion is narrow. Let the actual uncertainty in front of you decide, not a proxy like how new the product is. Avoid running several overlapping canvases on the same decision just to seem thorough.

## What a good teaching exchange produces
One lesson drawn from a real (or the user's own) example → a limitation or counterexample to it → a provisional equivalent stated for the user's own product → what evidence is still needed → the next concrete decision. Build on choices the user has already made rather than starting over. A clear example should make the user's own judgment easier to exercise, not substitute for it.
