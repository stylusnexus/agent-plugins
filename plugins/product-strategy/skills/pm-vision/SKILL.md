# Mission, Vision, and Winning Aspiration

Read [shared principles](../product-manager/references/principles.md) before applying this workflow independently. Use only the sections needed for the current decision.

## Purpose
Help choose a meaningful direction before generating slogans or a feature plan. Use these working definitions, while respecting an organization's existing vocabulary:
- **Mission:** why the product exists, whom it serves, and the contribution it makes today.
- **Vision:** the better future for those people that the product seeks to enable.
- **Winning aspiration:** what meaningful success would look like in the chosen arena, for customers and the builder. Make it more specific than being the best.
- **Strategy:** the connected choices for pursuing that aspiration. Move into the strategy work for those choices (internally `pm-strategy`) — describe that to the user as "next we work out where to compete and how to win," not by the skill's file name.

These are working distinctions, not universal definitions. Roger Martin accepts purpose/vision/mission as alternative labels for the motivating aspiration and cautions against redundant statements. Honor the user's vocabulary and use multiple statements only if they improve decisions. Link ambition to defined, observable progress.

## Workflow
1. Read the product's current purpose, capabilities, users, research, and constraints. Distinguish founder intent from observed customer need. For an idea without users, explicitly label customer claims as hypotheses.
2. Identify the beneficiary, their consequential problem, the change worth creating, and the builder's motivation and boundaries. Support commercial products, internal tools, open source, creative projects, and personal tools; do not impose revenue or market domination as universal goals.
3. Draft two or three meaningfully different directions if direction is unsettled. Explain whom each serves, the ambition it implies, and whom or what it excludes. Recommend one without silently deciding the user's purpose.
4. Draft a concise mission, vision, and winning aspiration that reinforce one another. If two statements say the same thing, explain the overlap and avoid manufacturing extra wording merely to fill a template.
5. Stress-test: Could a competitor say this unchanged? Is it merely a feature list, technology choice, or revenue target? Does it help reject an attractive distraction? Is the aspiration credible given constraints, even if ambitious?
6. Name observable signs of progress, without inventing a baseline or promising a result. Carry strategic choices into strategy work and measurement into objectives work next (internally `pm-strategy`, `pm-objectives`) — tell the user what that means in plain words, not the skill names.

## Output
- Context and evidence, with uncertainties.
- Proposed mission, vision, and winning aspiration; one or two sentences each.
- Alternatives and why the recommended direction fits.
- Boundaries: what this direction does not require or justify.
- Next decision and the evidence that could change the recommendation.

## Teach while working
Explain one relevant distinction using the actual draft. For example, distinguish a future user outcome from a business target. Offer a short revision exercise in workshop mode; do not require a quiz to finish useful work.

## Adaptable prompt
“Using the supplied evidence about this product and its intended users, help define its mission, vision, and winning aspiration. Compare plausible directions, explain their implications, and distinguish ambitions from proven customer needs. Recommend concise statements that guide real choices. Do not turn the vision into a feature list.”
