# Product leadership: situational plays

General management practice written for product leadership moments, not a single book
extraction. Where a technique has a well-known public originator it's named as further
reading — Kim Scott's *Radical Candor* (2017) for balancing personal care with direct
challenge, Hersey & Blanchard's situational leadership model for matching support to task
familiarity, Daniel Pink's *Drive* (2009) for autonomy/mastery/purpose, and Amy Edmondson's
research on psychological safety — but the plays themselves are ordinary practice, not a
proprietary framework being reproduced. See [sources](../../product-manager/references/sources.md#teams-and-operating-model)
for the primary links.

For a solo builder or agent-assisted workflow, translate "team" into "the responsible person or
agent" and keep the same discipline; don't simulate an organization that doesn't exist.

## Giving someone real ownership

Before any handoff, be explicit about the customer problem, how it ties to strategy, the
objective, known constraints, existing evidence, and who decides what. Write it down rather than
trust it survived a verbal handoff. State what's owned outright, what needs a check-in first,
and how you'll know it went well — ownership without that context is just leaving someone to
guess.

## Coaching through something unfamiliar

Ask what specifically feels hard and get a real example, not a general complaint. Explain the
missing concept, then work the next step together rather than handing over the answer. Match
scaffolding to task familiarity and stakes — a judgment call about the situation, not a label
assigned to the person once. Agree a small, low-stakes place to practice and a follow-up.

## Disagreeing productively

Name the decision and the outcome everyone wants, then separate observation from interpretation
from preference. Invite the dissenting view before defending your own, and restate every
position fairly enough its holder would agree you got it right. Compare real trade-offs, decide
within whoever's actual authority covers it, and record the reasoning, what's unresolved, and
what would prompt revisiting it. Being open to challenge isn't the same as needing unanimous
agreement — someone still decides, and being clear who that is up front avoids a later fight
about process.

## Delegating with real boundaries

Agree what's genuinely owned, what resources and dependencies come with it, what guardrails
apply, and a check-in rhythm sized to the risk. Resist prescribing implementation detail just to
feel in control — that instinct usually means the boundaries or the trust weren't clear enough
going in. Keep expertise boundaries honest: directing engineering's technical approach or
design's craft decisions isn't delegation, it's overreach, however well-intentioned.

## When something goes wrong

Handle urgent harm first, through whatever incident process exists. Once stable, examine the
evidence, the decisions made, the conditions people were actually working under, and what's
learned — without inventing motives nobody stated. A reasonable bet that didn't pay off and a
commitment that was simply ignored call for different responses. Make one concrete change, and
credit the people who handled it well accurately.

## Deciding without full information

Lay out the real options and the cost of delay. Prefer a reversible choice when one exists.
State explicitly what evidence would change your mind, and share the reasoning, not just the
call. Decisiveness isn't the same as being unwilling to update on new evidence. And don't
mistake confidence or a polished pitch for proof someone is right — the two are independent.

## Keeping the operation healthy

At a fitting cadence, check honestly against two public models rather than inventing a checklist
from scratch. Daniel Pink's *Drive* names autonomy, mastery, and purpose as the three drivers of
sustained motivation: can people actually shape the work (autonomy)? Is support available when
someone's learning something new, so they can visibly get better at it (mastery)? Is the purpose
clear enough to explain why the work matters (purpose)? Amy Edmondson's psychological safety adds
a fourth, load-bearing check: is it actually safe to raise a concern, ask a question, or admit a
mistake without being punished or humiliated for it? Adapt the format to the real team rather than
running a ritual because it's scheduled. Recognize progress and share credit accurately, and don't
assume one motivation or one productivity claim holds for everyone.

---

Outputs might be a context brief, coaching plan, decision log, facilitation outline, or
retrospective. Draft messages and never send them, and never take a real personnel action,
without explicit authorization from whoever's accountable for that call — these are thinking
tools, not delegated authority.
