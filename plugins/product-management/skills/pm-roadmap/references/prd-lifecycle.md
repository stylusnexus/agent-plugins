# Treating the initiative brief as a living decision record

The [initiative brief](../templates/initiative-prd.md) exists to support one thing: a specific decision, made by a specific person, on a specific timeline. Everything in it should serve that — not stand as a permanent spec, and not be filled in just because the template has a section for it. Use it for work substantial or uncertain enough that shared context genuinely helps; a two-line fix doesn't need one.

## Open it when there's a real decision to support

Start the brief once there's a real problem, some evidence, and a decision on the horizon — even before the solution is settled. The earliest sections (the decision needed, the problem, the evidence) are the ones least likely to need rewriting later, because they describe reality rather than a plan. Product, design and engineering should all be able to see themselves in it. A document that everyone has read is not the same thing as agreement — flag a consequential open question for a real conversation instead of assuming silence means consent.

## Keep it current as the decision changes

As discovery, prototyping or early delivery surfaces new information, update the problem framing, the options, and the recommendation in place, rather than starting a second document. Link out to experiments, prototypes, and detailed technical specs instead of copying their content into the brief, so there's one place each piece of information is true. When an assumption is invalidated by later learning, mark the brief's claim as superseded and say what replaced it — don't leave contradictory text for the next reader to puzzle over. Rejecting the option entirely, or reframing it substantially, is a legitimate outcome of this process, not a failure of it.

## Size the detail to the decision's stakes

A brief for a reversible, low-cost decision can stay short. A brief in front of a hard-to-reverse commitment, a cross-team dependency, or a firm external date earns more detail: explicit options considered, named risks with owners, and acceptance criteria precise enough that "done" isn't a judgment call later. Let the actual stakes decide the level of detail, not a fixed length rule. Always distinguish a date the organization has actually committed to from a date that's still an estimate.

## Don't let "done" or "when" stay implicit

Acceptance criteria and dates are the two places a brief most often stays vague without anyone noticing. Write acceptance criteria specific enough that whether the smallest first slice is actually done doesn't need a judgment call later. Put every date in its own table, one row each, each marked estimate or commitment — a date mentioned only in prose tends to drift into being read as a commitment nobody actually made. Keep a short rejected/deferred list next to the recommendation; an option that lost is still worth a one-line record of why, both for the current reader and for whoever revisits this later. If diagnosis of the underlying problem turns up several plausible causes and none has actually been isolated, that's a stop condition, not a detail to paper over — say so and propose the smallest step that would isolate it before committing to a fix.

## Don't duplicate, and don't leave gaps

If launch, sales, support or training materials already exist elsewhere, link to them instead of re-describing them. Where no specialist owns that readiness work — a small team without a dedicated marketing or support function, say — don't use that absence as an excuse to skip the decision; capture the relevant readiness questions directly in the brief so nothing falls through.

## Working with the user

For someone new to writing these, walk through what each section is for and draft a first pass from whatever evidence already exists in the conversation, rather than handing over a blank form. For someone who already knows what they want, produce the concise brief directly and label the genuine unknowns rather than guessing to fill every field. When the output needs review from other people, use this plugin's redline/artifact workflow so feedback lands as marked-up changes, not a second freeform document.
