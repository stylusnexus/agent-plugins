# Build and review an outcome roadmap

Source: Paweł Huryn, *3 Ways to Create 10X Better Product Roadmaps*, December 27, 2022. Source originals are not included; use the bundled guidance and Markdown worksheets. The guide and worksheet below are original operational adaptations. The source diagram credits Janna Bastow for Now-Next-Later. No claim of a measured tenfold improvement is adopted.

## Coach through three decisions

1. **Choose the change that matters.** Read the accepted strategy and objective. Ask what should become better for the customer and why that matters to the organization. Convert a feature request into a problem and observable outcome; retain the feature as a possible solution. Do not invent a metric baseline or assume the solution will cause the outcome.
2. **Choose the next responsible commitment.** Review value, usability, viability, feasibility and ethics risks, alongside capacity, dependencies and delivery uncertainty. A learning commitment (for example, observe a workflow and decide whether to proceed) can be more appropriate than promising delivery. Record any real deadline, its reason, owner and consequences. Keep commitments, estimates and planning horizons distinct.
3. **Choose how much detail is justified.** Use Now for active, capacity-supported work; Next for plausible priorities with conditions to start; Later for tentative problems or opportunities. These are not automatically calendar quarters. Give distant work less detail and identify what would promote, defer or stop it.

## Connect the layers

Show strategic choice → product outcome → customer need → candidate intervention → evidence and next decision. This is a proposed causal relationship until supported by evidence. A roadmap communicates direction and choices; a backlog holds detailed possible work; a release plan handles delivery coordination.

A solution can appear when useful, clearly marked as a hypothesis or an accepted commitment. Do not conceal agreed scope from stakeholders merely to keep a roadmap feature-free. Include discovery and outcome measurement, not only delivery.

## Evidence and commitment safeguards

- Discovery does not certify an entire backlog. Evidence is scoped to the people, conditions and risks actually tested; revisit it as conditions change.
- Do not adopt the source's brain-hemisphere explanation, three-month anecdote, or cone-of-uncertainty reference as universal laws.
- Estimates and team conditions can be investigated and improved; uncertainty remains. The source's claim that discovery cannot mitigate these at all is too absolute.
- A genuine external commitment is not erased by calling it an estimate. Communicate risk, renegotiate explicitly if needed, and preserve the decision trail.
- Do not copy the example's targets into a real product or infer any numeric probability of success.
- The source mentions commitments in “Now or Later”; do not turn this into a horizon rule. Judge evidence, capacity and obligations for the actual item.

## Review and visuals

At a proportionate review interval or when material evidence changes, compare observed results with the intended outcome. Decide continue, change, stop or investigate. Preserve rationale for moved items and communicate effects on accepted commitments.

For a visual, route to pm-visuals: Lucid preferred when available, Mermaid fallback. Use three horizon columns with outcome and customer-need lanes; add evidence, dependencies and decision notes. Keep the working table as accessible text. Visual placement does not imply a delivery date.
