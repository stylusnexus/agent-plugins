# Running OKRs as a cadence, not a form

Grounded in Andy Grove's Intel management-by-objectives practice (*High Output Management*,
1983), which built key results onto Peter Drucker's management by objectives (*The Practice of
Management*, [1954](https://archive.org/details/practiceofmanage00druc); [history](https://www.whatmatters.com/articles/the-origin-story), which places it "in the 1950s"), John Doerr's account of Objectives and Key Results (*Measure What Matters*, 2018), and
Christina Wodtke's continuous-cycle refinement (*Radical Focus*) — independent public
sources describing overlapping practice, not one fixed spec.

## The objective

Qualitative and time-bound: a meaningful shift the team wants true by period's end, stated so an
outsider would understand why it matters. "Ship the redesigned settings page" is a task wearing
an objective's clothes; "make billing changes something people trust enough not to call
support" points at an outcome. Keep the count small — one to three per team is a common range,
not a rule; a team drowning in objectives usually has a prioritization problem in disguise.

## Key results

The evidence the objective happened, not the build list. Each needs the exact metric and how
it's computed, the cohort, a baseline (or an explicit "unknown — this cycle is discovery"), a
target with real reasoning behind it, a deadline, and an owner. Separate delivery key results
("ship X") from outcome key results ("X moves by Y") when both appear — finishing a build isn't
proof of the value promised. Co-write key results with the people doing the work; targets handed
down without that conversation get quietly reinterpreted mid-cycle.

## Setting the cycle up

Agree who owns the objective, how much latitude they have to change approach, what it depends
on, review frequency, and which guardrails must not silently deteriorate while key results
climb. A target with no stated rationale and a baseline nobody checked are the two most common
reasons a cycle's "confidence" turns out to be theater.

## The check-in

Reuse an existing cadence rather than inventing a meeting. Cover: current value against baseline
and target, whether the data is fresh and trustworthy, a confidence read with the reasoning
behind it (confidence is judgment, not a percentage of tasks closed), guardrail status, what was
learned, blockers, and the next decision. Write down why a target, scope, or approach changed —
silently moving the goalposts defeats the point of having them. Decide guardrail-breach
responses in advance, scaled to severity, rather than arguing about them live.

## Closing the cycle

Compare the outcome honestly to where things started, name what's still uncertain and any
unanticipated side effects, and make an explicit continue/adapt/stop call. Contribution isn't
proof of causation — say which one you actually have. If the objective no longer serves the
strategy it came from, say so instead of rolling it forward by default.

## Fitting the practice to context

Cascade intent, not arithmetic — splitting a company number evenly by percentage usually
misattributes credit and blame, since most teams don't independently control it. Preserve real
dependencies between teams. When the real job is reducing uncertainty, a learning objective is
fine, but state up front what evidence and decision it should produce, not just an interview
count. Not every workstream needs an OKR, and using OKRs as an individual performance scorecard
tends to produce sandbagged targets rather than honest ones.

For a solo builder or agent-assisted workflow, keep the rigor with a lighter footprint: one
owner, one honest baseline, one review date, same rule about not counting delivered work as
proof of outcome.

## A lightweight check-in record, if useful

Objective and the strategy it serves; each key result's baseline/current/target/source/date;
guardrail status; confidence and reasoning; what changed since last review; next action and
owner; next review date. Use only as much as the work needs.
