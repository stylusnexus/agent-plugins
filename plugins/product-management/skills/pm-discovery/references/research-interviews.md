# Running a research interview that actually finds things out

This supports preparing for and making sense of interviews — not unsolicited recruitment, and not recording anyone without their say.

## The core rule: past behavior, not hypothetical intent
Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (<https://www.momtestbook.com/>) is built on one discipline: people are unreliable predictors of what they'd do in a hypothetical future, but accurate reporters of what they actually did. "Would you use this?" invites a polite, optimistic guess; "walk me through the last time you tried to do this" produces a fact that's harder to get wrong.

Teresa Torres teaches the same discipline through story-based questions (<https://www.producttalk.org/2022/04/best-customer-interview-questions/>): humans "are bad at accurately answering direct questions out of context," so instead of a direct question about preferences, ask them to describe a specific recent instance. Her own worked example swaps a direct question — "What do you like to watch?" — for a story-based one: "Tell me about the last time you watched Netflix." From there, this plugin's own guidance is to pull chronological detail with targeted follow-ups: what triggered it, what happened next, what they tried, what got in the way. A story anchored to a specific recent episode surfaces the real obstacles and workarounds that a direct question about preferences would not.

## Before the interview
Write down the product decision and the real question behind it — a research question and a conversational question aren't the same thing. Keep needs discovery separate from usability testing. Choose participants and channels deliberately, noting selection bias, missed segments, and any incentive offered. Drafting outreach is fine; sending it, or offering payment, needs its own authorization. Agreeing to an interview isn't automatic consent to being recorded, transcribed, or having the conversation reused widely.

## Running it
Open by explaining purpose, confirming participation is voluntary, agreeing on recording, and making clear they can skip or stop. Ask one or two neutral prompts anchored to a specific recent episode, with follow-ups for the trigger, what happened next, alternatives, and any workaround used. Let the participant correct a wrong interpretation rather than defending it — imperfect recall or a stated opinion isn't dishonesty. Listen for what's surprising and follow it, rather than steering back to a script.

Don't pitch the idea being tested mid-conversation, and don't praise it either — both pull the story toward flattering the interviewer instead of reporting what happened. A deliberate concept-test question has its place, but it's a different kind of interview from a story-based discovery one; don't blend them without deciding to.

## Closing and write-up
Close by asking what was missed, summarizing back for correction, and asking separately about future contact. Keep contact info and retention to what was actually agreed. Write an evidence-linked summary: participant ID/context, episode timeline, job/desired outcome, actions and workarounds, reported thoughts/feelings, problems/consequences, counterevidence, limitations, and the next research decision. Keep a direct quote distinct from paraphrase or interpretation, and don't invent an emotion, an importance score, a date, or an owner that wasn't actually said. Carry source IDs into a discovery digest, journey map, or opportunity analysis.

Teach by drafting one real story-anchored prompt and explaining what it's likely to surface that a direct question wouldn't. A finding is a perspective from one conversation, not measured prevalence or proven causation, and a rehearsed AI-simulated conversation is synthetic practice, never a stand-in for a real participant.
